## [00:00 – 00:50] Cold Open: The Impossible Photograph

[SFX: Gentle slosh of liquid in a darkroom tray. A mechanical 35mm camera shutter clicks.]

**HOST:**
Picture yourself standing alone in a darkroom under a dim red bulb. You've just developed a roll of black-and-white film you shot earlier that afternoon—just a quiet patch of grass outside a shopping center. Nothing strange. You hold the wet strip of negatives up to the light.

[PAUSE 1.0s]

And right across the middle of the frame, burned into the negative in sharp white digits, is a number: twelve hundred, zero zero, zero zero.

You slide to the next frame. Eleven ninety-nine, forty-nine, thirty-three. The third frame: eleven ninety-nine, forty, eighteen. Every single photograph you take, no matter where you aim the camera, has a clock ticking down to zero.

[SFX: Faint, steady electronic tick—once per second, then fading.]

And then you blink—and realize the numbers aren't on the film anymore. They're floating inside your own eyes.

## [00:50 – 02:15] Context & Stakes: Two Timelines Collide

**HOST:**
That nightmare happens to Wang Miao, a nanomaterials researcher in Beijing, in the opening chapters of Cixin Liu's *The Three-Body Problem*. If you've only heard the title and assumed it's a dry astrophysics puzzle, it isn't. It's a conspiracy that spans half a century.

The story doesn't open with spaceships or alien invasions. It opens in Beijing in 1967, at the height of the Cultural Revolution. A Tsinghua University physics professor named Ye Zhetai is dragged onto a wooden stage in front of a roaring crowd of Red Guards. His crime is teaching relativity, quantum mechanics, and Big Bang cosmology. His own wife, Shao Lin, steps up to denounce him. Ye Zhetai refuses to back down—and he is beaten to death right there on stage while his daughter, an astrophysicist named Ye Wenjie, watches helplessly from the crowd.

[PAUSE 1.0s]

That afternoon breaks Ye Wenjie's faith in humanity. After years in a brutal labor corps in Inner Mongolia—and nearly dying in a freezing prison cell after being framed for owning a copy of *Silent Spring*—she is pulled out of obscurity and sent to a top-secret military radar base on a remote mountain peak. The base is called Red Coast. And what she discovers inside that radar dish will set a forty-year trap for the entire human species.

## [02:15 – 05:15] The Dead Physicists & The Pool Table

[SFX: Distant city traffic fading into a quiet indoor room.]

**HOST:**
Jump forward decades to the present day. Around the world, top theoretical physicists are taking their own lives. One of them is Yang Dong—who happens to be Ye Wenjie's own daughter. Yang Dong leaves behind a short suicide note. Listen to her exact words from the novel:

[SLOW] *"All the evidence points to a single conclusion: Physics has never existed, and will never exist. I know what I'm doing is irresponsible. But I have no choice."*

[PAUSE 1.5s]

"Physics has never existed, and will never exist." What could possibly convince a world-class physicist that the laws of nature are an illusion?

Wang Miao finds out from Ding Yi, a physicist who was engaged to Yang Dong. Ding Yi brings Wang to a quiet pool table. He lines up a white cue ball and a black ball, takes a shot, and sinks the black ball cleanly into the corner pocket. Classic Newtonian physics. Then Ding Yi asks him: what if we carried this exact pool table to another room, hit the exact same shot, and the black ball flew straight up to the ceiling? What if we moved it a third time, and the ball vanished into thin air?

[SFX: Sharp crack of billiard balls colliding, echoing slightly.]

That is what's happening inside the world's largest particle accelerators. Scientists run the exact same collision experiment under identical conditions, and every time, the subatomic debris scatters in completely random, contradictory directions. The foundation of all science—that nature follows consistent rules—has suddenly broken.

And that's when Shi Qiang walks into Wang Miao's life. Everyone calls him Da Shi. He's a rough, chain-smoking Beijing police detective working with a joint military-intelligence group called the Battle Command Center. Da Shi doesn't pretend to understand particle physics, and he doesn't get rattled by famous professors. He spots a pattern: the dead scientists were all tied to a shadowy group called the Frontiers of Science. And now, the group's recruiter, Shen Yufei, has started circling Wang Miao.

## [05:15 – 07:45] The Universe Flickers (Science Check)

**HOST:**
This is where the story takes a chilling turn. Shen Yufei warns Wang Miao to shut down his nanomaterial research—specifically his work on ultra-strong carbon fibers. When he refuses, the countdown appears in his retinas. Fifty days, ticking down second by second.

Desperate to make it stop, Wang Miao confronts Shen Yufei. She makes him an offer that sounds like pure madness: if you still don't believe there's a power beyond human science, wait until one o'clock tomorrow morning. The entire universe is going to flicker for you.

Wang calls an astronomer friend, Dr. Sha Ruishan, whose lab receives live sky data from three space observatories: COBE, WMAP, and Planck. Together, they measure the Cosmic Microwave Background—the faint thermal afterglow of the Big Bang. Wang asks Sha to watch for an isotropic fluctuation across the entire sky of one to five percent. Sha laughs out loud. He tells Wang that the overall microwave background only shifts over the lifespan of the universe itself. A five-percent jump in a single night would mean—in Sha's words—*"the universe would flicker like a fluorescent tube that's about to burn out!"*

[SFX: Steady, soft monitor hum. Suddenly, a sharp digital chime at `01:00`, followed by rapid, erratic static pulses.]

At one a.m. sharp, the green line on Dr. Sha's terminal goes haywire. Across the entire sky, in every direction, the cosmic background radiation surges up and down by five percent—blinking out a Morse code pattern that matches Wang Miao's retinal countdown down to the second.

[PAUSE 1.0s]

Now let's pause for a quick science check. Is the Cosmic Microwave Background real? Yes, completely. Discovered in 1964 by Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, the CMB is genuine relic radiation from about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, sitting at a uniform temperature of 2.725 Kelvin. But here's the catch: in real physics, the entire observable universe cannot blink in unison—because light itself takes billions of years to travel from one side of the sky to the other. Which means whatever happened at one a.m. wasn't the cosmos breaking. Someone—or something—was hijacking Wang Miao's eyes and Dr. Sha's instruments at the same time.

## [07:45 – 10:45] Inside *Three Body*: Dehydrate!

[SFX: Dry desert wind whistling across open stone.]

**HOST:**
Terrified, Wang Miao shuts down his lab, and the countdown disappears from his vision. Still, he can't walk away. With Da Shi backing him up, Wang logs into a strange, invitation-only virtual reality game called *Three Body*.

He slips into a haptic V-suit and pulls on the visor. Instantly, he's standing on a barren, freezing plain under a pale sky. Walking beside him are two figures dressed in robes from ancient Chinese history: King Wen of Zhou and a exhausted follower.

It sounds bizarre—ancient philosophers inside a sci-fi game—until you realize the game is using human history as a metaphor for an alien world's survival horror.

In this world, there are no reliable seasons. Sometimes the sun stays down for days, freezing the air solid. Sometimes it blazes so close that lakes boil away. These are **Chaotic Eras**. Civilization can only grow during a **Stable Era**—rare, precious windows of mild, twenty-hour days.

So how do people survive when hell breaks loose? They expel every drop of water from their bodies, shrivel into dry, flat leather hides, and get rolled up and stacked inside stone vaults called dehydratories. When a Stable Era finally arrives, soldiers carry the dried bodies down to a lake and toss them in. As the skins hit the water, they soak it up, swell back into flesh and bone, and stand up—ready to rebuild civilization from zero until the next catastrophe strikes.

[PAUSE 1.0s]

Wang Miao watches an entire empire rehydrate, enjoy eight brief days of gentle spring, and then watch in horror as two suns rise together and turn the earth into an oven. A rider gallops through the crowd screaming at the top of his lungs: *"Dehydrate! Dehydrate!"* Those who can't dry out in time burst into flames like logs thrown into a furnace.

And here is the real physics behind that nightmare. Isaac Newton solved the two-body gravitational problem cleanly—two objects orbiting each other follow predictable ellipses. Add a third star, and the math explodes. In the late nineteenth century, French mathematician Henri Poincaré proved there is no general closed-form solution for three bodies pulling on each other. It's deterministic chaos: the tiniest nudge changes the entire future of the orbit. Even more remarkably, our closest neighboring star system—Alpha Centauri, four light-years away—really *is* a triple-star system, and in 2016 astronomers confirmed an Earth-sized exoplanet there called Proxima Centauri b. In reality, Proxima orbits far enough from the other two stars to stay stable. In Cixin Liu's novel, those three suns dance close enough to scorch and freeze a world without warning.

## [10:45 – 12:00] Climax & Cliffhanger: The Message From 1979

[SFX: Low, resonant radio static slowly fading in.]

**HOST:**
So what connects a dying alien world around three suns to dead physicists in Beijing, a countdown in Wang Miao's eyes, and a blinking sky?

Go back to Ye Wenjie at Red Coast Base. While studying solar physics in secret, she discovered that if you aim a powerful enough radio beam at the Sun, its internal layers act like a cosmic amplifier. In 1971, she pointed the dish straight at the Sun and sent a message from Earth out into the galaxy.

Eight years later, in 1979, she was sitting alone at her console when the receiver hummed to life. A reply had arrived from four light-years away. It wasn't a friendly hello. It was a desperate warning from a lone pacifist listener on a doomed world:

[SLOW] *"Do not answer! Do not answer! Do not answer!"*

[PAUSE 1.0s]

"Do not answer! If you reply, your world will be conquered!"

Ye Wenjie stared at that message on her screen. She remembered her father beaten to death on a university stage. She remembered the cruelty, the betrayal, the endless cycle of human destruction. And then, she reached for the console—and pressed transmit anyway.

[SFX: Single decisive switch click. Deep bass swell cuts abruptly to silence.]

In Episode Two, the countdown hits the real world. We'll step inside a living computer built from thirty million soldiers, uncover the secret society preparing Earth for an alien fleet, and witness one of the most ruthless ambushes in modern sci-fi: Operation Guzheng. I'll see you there.

That nightmare happens to Wang Miao, a nanomaterials researcher in Beijing, right at the start of Cixin Liu's *The Three-Body Problem*. And if you think this is just a creepy psychological thriller, wait until you see what that countdown is actually tied to.

That moment breaks something inside Ye Wenjie. After years in a brutal labor corps in Inner Mongolia—and nearly dying in prison after being framed for owning a copy of Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring*—she is pulled out of the cold and sent to a secret military radar base high on a mountain peak. ## [02:15 – 05:15] The Dead Physicists & The Pool Table

[VISUAL: B-roll of a massive particle collider tunnel, then cut to a billiard table with a white cue ball and a black eight-ball.]

**HOST:**
Jump forward decades to the present day. Leading theoretical physicists around the world are dying by suicide. One of them is Yang Dong—who, quietly, happens to be Ye Wenjie's own daughter. Before she dies, Yang Dong leaves behind a short, chilling note. Here are her exact words from the novel:

[ON-SCREEN TEXT: *"All the evidence points to a single conclusion: Physics has never existed, and will never exist. I know what I'm doing is irresponsible. But I have no choice."*]

**HOST:**
"Physics has never existed, and will never exist." What could make a brilliant scientist believe that the laws of nature themselves are fake?

Wang Miao gets his answer from Ding Yi, a physicist and Yang Dong's grieving fiancé. Ding Yi takes Wang to a pool table. He lines up a white cue ball and a black ball, shoots, and sinks the black ball into the pocket. Simple Newtonian mechanics, right? Then Ding Yi asks Wang a terrifying question: what if you moved the table to a different room, and the exact same shot sent the black ball flying straight up to the ceiling? What if you moved it a third time, and the ball vanished?

That is what's happening inside the world's most powerful particle accelerators. Physicists run the exact same collision experiment under identical conditions, and every single time, the subatomic debris scatters in completely random, contradictory ways. The bedrock of science—repeatability—has just collapsed.

[VISUAL: Detective **Shi Qiang (Da Shi)** character card; smoky interrogation room aesthetic.]

**HOST:**
Enter Shi Qiang—everyone calls him Da Shi—a blunt, chain-smoking Beijing police detective working with a global military coalition called the Battle Command Center. Da Shi doesn't care about quantum theory, and he doesn't get intimidated by Nobel-level academics. He notices a pattern: many of the dead physicists were connected to a mysterious group called the Frontiers of Science. And now, the group's recruiter, Shen Yufei, has her eyes on Wang Miao.

## [05:15 – 07:45] The Universe Flickers (Science Check)

[VISUAL: Radio telescope array at night; then animated 2.7 K Cosmic Microwave Background sky map (COBE / WMAP / Planck).]

**HOST:**
Here's where the story turns genuinely eerie. Shen Yufei warns Wang Miao to shut down his nanomaterial research—specifically his work on ultra-strong carbon fibers. When he refuses, the retinal countdown appears in his vision. Fifty days left. Counting down every second.

Wang Miao goes back to Shen Yufei, desperate to make it stop. She tells him: if you still don't believe in a power beyond human science, wait until one o'clock tomorrow morning. The universe itself is going to flicker for you.

Wang visits an astronomer friend, Dr. Sha Ruishan, whose lab monitors real-time data from three space observatories: COBE, WMAP, and Planck. They're measuring the Cosmic Microwave Background—the faint afterglow of the Big Bang. Wang tells Sha he expects to see an isotropic fluctuation across the entire sky of one to five percent. Sha laughs in his face. He explains that the overall CMB only shifts on the timescale of the age of the universe. A five-percent jump in one night would mean, in Sha's words, *"the universe would flicker like a fluorescent tube that's about to burn out!"*

[SFX / MUSIC: Sharp electronic beep. Monitor line spikes.]

[VISUAL: Flat green telemetry line suddenly spiking violently at `01:00:00`.]

**HOST:**
At one a.m. sharp, the green line on Dr. Sha's screen goes wild. The entire sky—every direction in the cosmos—pulses up and down by five percent, blinking out a Morse code pattern that matches Wang Miao's countdown down to the second.

[ON-SCREEN TEXT: `SCIENCE CHECK: COSMIC MICROWAVE BACKGROUND (CMB)`]

**HOST:**
Okay, quick science check. Is the Cosmic Microwave Background real? One hundred percent yes. Discovered in 1964 by Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, the CMB is actual thermal radiation left over from roughly 380,000 years after the Big Bang. Its temperature is a remarkably uniform 2.725 Kelvin, with tiny ripples of about one part in a hundred thousand. In real physics, nothing short of magic could make the entire observable universe's microwave background blink by five percent in unison—because light itself takes billions of years to cross those distances. Which raises the real question: if the universe *can't* physically flicker like that, *who* is hacking Wang Miao's eyes—and Dr. Sha's instruments?

## [07:45 – 10:45] Inside *Three Body*: Dehydrate!

[VISUAL: Futuristic haptic V-suit and VR visor; transition into a stark, barren desert under a cold, colorless sky.]

**HOST:**
Shaken to his core, Wang Miao shuts down his lab, and the countdown vanishes. But he isn't walking away. With Da Shi watching his back, Wang logs into a strange, invitation-only virtual reality game recommended by Shen Yufei's circle. It's called *Three Body*.

He puts on a haptic V-suit and visor, and steps into a world that feels brutally, physically real. It's a desolate plain under a dim twilight sky. Walking beside him are two characters from ancient Chinese history: King Wen of Zhou and a weary follower.

Wait—why are ancient Chinese philosophers wandering through a sci-fi wasteland? Because the game uses human history as a skin for an alien planet's nightmare.

[VISUAL: Animation of people rolling up into flat, dry fibrous sheets and being stacked inside stone vaults.]

**HOST:**
In this world, there are no predictable seasons. Sometimes the sun doesn't rise for days, and the air freezes solid. Sometimes it blazes so close that oceans boil. These are **Chaotic Eras**. Civilization can only survive when a **Stable Era** arrives—a brief window of mild, twenty-hour days.

And how do the people of this world survive the Chaotic Eras? They expel all the water from their bodies, roll up like dry leather hides, and stack themselves inside stone warehouses called dehydratories. When a Stable Era begins, soldiers toss the dried bodies into a lake. They soak up water, unfurl, and wake up—ready to rebuild civilization from scratch until the next apocalypse hits.

[ON-SCREEN TEXT: `"DEHYDRATE!"`]

**HOST:**
Wang Miao watches King Zhou's empire wake up, enjoy eight peaceful days of spring, and then—without warning—collapse again as two stars rise together and turn the sky into a furnace. The scream echoes across the plain: *"Dehydrate! Dehydrate!"* People who don't dry out in time catch fire like logs in a stove.

[ON-SCREEN TEXT: `SCIENCE CHECK: THE THREE-BODY PROBLEM`]

**HOST:**
Here's the physics behind the horror. Isaac Newton solved the two-body gravitational problem cleanly—two objects orbiting each other trace predictable ellipses. Add a third star, and everything breaks. In the late nineteenth century, mathematician Henri Poincaré proved there is no general closed-form equation for three bodies pulling on each other. It's deterministic chaos: a tiny nudge changes the entire future of the orbit. And in real life, our closest neighboring star system—Alpha Centauri, just four light-years away—actually *is* a triple-star system, with an Earth-sized exoplanet called Proxima Centauri b confirmed there in 2016. In reality, Proxima orbits far enough away from the other two stars to stay stable. In the novel, those three suns dance close enough to tear a world apart.

## [10:45 – 12:00] Climax & Cliffhanger: The Message From 1979

[VISUAL: Cut back to Red Coast Base in 1971/1979—a towering dish antenna pointed straight at the setting sun.]

**HOST:**
So what does a dying alien world in a chaotic three-star system have to do with dead physicists in Beijing, a countdown in Wang Miao's eyes, and a blinking sky?

Remember Ye Wenjie at Red Coast Base? While studying solar physics, she realized that if you fire a strong enough radio signal at the Sun, its internal layers amplify the broadcast across interstellar space. In 1971, she secretly aimed the dish at the Sun and transmitted a greeting from Earth.

Eight years later, in 1979, she was sitting alone at the console when the receiver hummed. A message came back from four light-years away. Not a greeting. A desperate warning from a lone pacifist alien listener on a dying world:

[ON-SCREEN TEXT: *"DO NOT ANSWER! DO NOT ANSWER! DO NOT ANSWER!"*]

**HOST:**
"Do not answer! If you reply, your world will be conquered!"

Ye Wenjie stared at that warning. She thought about her father beaten to death on a stage. She thought about human cruelty, greed, and betrayal. And then, she pressed the transmit button anyway.
